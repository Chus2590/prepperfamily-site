Module I

The Inalienable School and the Biological Vehicle

1.1 When the Connection Goes Down

Let's imagine a real situation at home: the internet connection fails or the power supply is interrupted for several days. Most modern school materials remain locked behind passwords or digital platforms. The family then discovers an unnoticed dependency that brings learning to a halt.

Would our children know how to keep learning without that digital connection?

Storing files on a screen is not the same as understanding reality. True education does not depend on electronic devices; it is the inalienable ability to observe, reason, investigate, and apply knowledge using the mind, the senses, and one's own body.

1.2 The Biological Vehicle

Before opening any textbook, the first "school" and the first "laboratory" a human being lives with is their own body. To lay the groundwork for Unani medicine and the understanding of constitutional biotypes, we teach our children the concept of the biological vehicle:

≈ 70% Factory Biology
≈ 30% Learned

≈ 70% · Factory Biology (the Biotype)

It is the unchangeable constitution each person arrives in the world with. It comes in a single, integrated package:

  • Anatomy and morphology: the bone architecture, the shape of the face, the proportions between torso and limbs, the muscle pattern, and the natural distribution of fat.
  • Physiology: the way the body processes energy, oxygen, temperature, water, and electrolytes.
  • Spontaneous temperament: the nervous system's natural response to environmental stimuli (initiative, exploration, calm observation, or relational empathy).

≈ 30% · Learned Experience (Personality)

It is the habitual way of functioning a person develops throughout life. It is built from family upbringing, lived events, training, acquired skills, and adaptation to circumstances.

Family pedagogical value. No vehicle is superior to another. A sports car, a cargo truck, or a compact car have different factory designs for different functions. When parents and children understand that factory biology determines nearly 70% of their physical and physiological tendencies, destructive comparisons between siblings disappear, and the foundations are laid for self-care adapted to each body's real needs.

1.3 Basic Morphology and Anatomy: The Entry Door

To identify the needs of our bodily vehicle, anatomy offers an observational entry door based on physical architecture:

  • Bone structure and proportions: observe whether the torso is longer or shorter relative to the limbs, the width of shoulders and hips, and the prominence of the joints.
  • Musculature and fat: distinguish between bodies with dense, factory-defined muscle mass, soft structures with rounded contours, or lean constitutions with little volume but high definition.
  • Facial geometry: observe the shape of the head and jaw (rectangular, triangular, square, or rounded) and the expression of the eyes.

This initial anatomical observation lays the groundwork for later understanding the 4 traditional Unani biotypes (Choleric/Fire, Sanguine/Air, Phlegmatic/Water, Melancholic/Earth), helping the family understand why not all children run at the same pace or need exactly the same foods or stimuli.

Module II

Biophysics and Physiology of Home Life

2.1 The Physiology of Energy: Combustion and Breathing

In the biophysical model of health, every illness or unexplained fatigue is, at bottom, a lack of available energy. For a student's brain and a child's muscles to work, the body needs to generate energy through a physiological process of combustion:

Nutrients (fats, proteins, sugars) + Oxygen (O2) ⟶ Energy (ATP) + Body heat (36.5 °C) + CO2 + H2O

  • The role of oxygen: we breathe between 15,000 and 25,000 liters of air per day. In the lung alveoli, red blood cells load up on oxygen to carry it to deep tissues. Without enough oxygen, combustion becomes inefficient and cells accumulate acidic waste.
  • Alkalization through breathing: as we exhale carbon dioxide (CO2), venous blood (pH ≈ 7.35) becomes more alkaline arterial blood (pH ≈ 7.45). Continuous breathing is the body's main mechanism for maintaining its pH balance second by second.

2.2 The Bioelectric System and Proper Hydration

A frequent mistake at home is to think of hydration as the simple act of "drinking lots of plain water." From human physiology and Unani medicine:

  1. The body as a bioelectric circuit: the brain, nerves, heart, and muscles transmit information through electrical impulses (membrane potentials of approximately −90 mV).
  2. The conductive medium: ionized salts and minerals (especially sodium, Na+, and chlorine, Cl−) are the "invisible wires" that allow current to flow. All body fluids (blood, tears, interstitial fluid) are saline and equivalent to an isotonic solution (~0.9% salinity).
  3. The danger of overhydration with plain water: drinking large amounts of demineralized water dilutes the electrolytes in the extracellular space, reduces bioelectric conductivity, and forces cells to absorb water by osmosis, causing cellular edema, headaches, heaviness, and mental fatigue.

2.3 Family Practice: Recipe and Protocol for the Daily Serum

To maintain the bioelectric conductivity of the brain and muscles during the home school day, the family will prepare the daily serum:

Daily Recipe

Pure water (filtered or boiled)
1 liter
Pure sea salt or unrefined marine salt
4 to 6 g per liter (approx. one level teaspoon)
Fresh lemon juice
Half a lemon or a whole lemon, to taste

Physiological Explanation for the Children

Sea salt provides the sodium chloride and trace minerals needed to restore the conductivity of body fluids, while lemon adds potassium, citrates, and improves the flavor. This serum prevents demineralization, quenches thirst for real, and keeps the mind clear without refined sugars or industrial drinks.

2.4 Biorhythms and Liver Physiology

The human body operates under circadian rhythms regulated by sunlight and darkness.

  • The liver as metabolic center: during the night, the liver performs critical tasks of cleansing, fat transformation, and preparation of hormonal precursors.
  • The early-dinner rule: if children eat a heavy dinner or eat very late (after 6:00 or 7:00 p.m.), they force the liver to spend its nighttime energy on heavy digestion, interfering with the natural production of melatonin. The result is restless sleep, nightmares, and waking up tired.
  • Light hygiene: dim bright artificial lights two hours before bed so the pineal gland can register the darkness and prepare the body for restorative rest.
Module III

The 4-Tool Pedagogical Method

To ensure learning stays active, deep, and screen-free, write the 4 study tools on a family card:

The 4-Tool Method

  1. ReadStudy a manageable passage.
  2. ExplainRetell in your own words or drawings.
  3. PracticeMeasure, calculate, or experiment.
  4. DemonstrateApply the idea to a new situation.

Step-by-Step Application in Anatomy and Physiology

  1. Read · entry step

    Action
    The parent or child reads a short passage (2 to 3 pages) about a system of the human body in a printed book or atlas.
    Example
    Read how red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to the muscles.

  2. Explain · ownership step

    Action
    The book is closed. The child explains what they understood without memorizing stock phrases, using drawings, diagrams, or everyday analogies.
    Example
    The child draws the heart and blood vessels, explaining: "Arteries carry oxygen-loaded blood like delivery trucks, and veins bring carbon dioxide back to breathe it out."

  3. Practice · body-laboratory step

    Action
    Carry out a hands-on measuring or direct experimentation activity at home.
    Example 1
    Measure heart rate (beats per minute) at rest, then measure it again after doing 20 squats, recording the difference in a chart.
    Example 2
    Prepare the daily serum, calculating the salt-to-water proportions per liter.

  4. Demonstrate · real-application step

    Action
    The child solves a new question or explains a real situation by applying the biological principle.
    Example
    Ask the child: "If an athlete runs for an hour under the sun and only drinks purified water without salt, why might they feel cramps or dizziness?" The child demonstrates understanding by explaining the bioelectric imbalance and sodium loss through sweat.
Module IV

The Home Library and the Home Laboratory

4.1 The Emergency Library: Six Essential Areas

Set up a bookshelf or wooden box protected from moisture, organized into 6 areas:

  1. Reading and WritingA dictionary of the language, a grammar and spelling manual with clear examples, a composition text, and classic literary works for reading aloud.
  2. MathematicsManuals of arithmetic, fractions, geometry, and problems applied to everyday life, with answer keys for the adult.
  3. Natural Sciences and BiologyAn illustrated human anatomy atlas, books on botany, basic zoology, physics, and meteorology.
  4. History and GeographyPhysical and political geographic atlases, timelines, and histories that explain causes and consequences.
  5. Practical KnowledgeReliable manuals on first aid, growing plants, sewing, food preservation, and basic carpentry.
  6. Moral Formation and ValuesBiographies of exemplary figures, universal classics, and texts for spiritual or family formation.

4.2 Turning Daily Chores into a Biological Laboratory

There is no need to turn every minute into a formal lesson; simply add one question, one measurement, and one record to the necessary household tasks:

In the Kitchen Physics, Chemistry, and Nutritional Density

  • Calculate half or double a recipe (fractions).
  • Observe how food transforms through heat or fermentation (like sourdough bread or vinegar).
  • Analyze nutritional density: compare the fullness produced by a meal rich in protein and healthy fats versus a meal based on refined carbohydrates.

In the Garden and Pots Botany and Minerals

  • Sow seeds in reused containers, measure daily growth, and record the variables of light, water, and soil.
  • Compare how plants absorb minerals from the soil analogously to how the body absorbs electrolytes.

In Rhythms and Rest Sleep Physiology

  • Record the time of the last meal and assess the quality of rest the next day.
  • Observe the impact of morning natural light on the day's energy level.
